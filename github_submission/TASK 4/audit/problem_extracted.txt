Background
The steam engine transformed industry by converting fuel into mechanical work. On the railways, 
this power had to be directed through a shared network. At a junction, a railway switch sends a train 
from one track to another. The route chosen affects journey time, congestion, and resource use, and 
the switch must be set before the train reaches the junction.
AI services face a similar decision. A routine request may be handled by a compact large language 
model (LLM), while a demanding task may require a more capable model or additional reasoning. At 
this computational switching point, the system must decide which path a request should take before it 
knows whether the answer will be satisfactory. Using the strongest model for every request can waste 
resources; relying only on the least expensive option can undermine answer quality.
These decisions also have environmental consequences. The electricity consumed by AI inference 
accumulates across millions of requests, while the carbon intensity of electricity varies between 
European regions and over time. Moving a computation to a lower-carbon location or waiting for 
cleaner electricity may reduce emissions, but can also increase response time. Moreover, a cheaper and 
more efficient AI service may attract enough additional demand to offset its expected environmental 
savings.
For this problem, imagine a hypothetical public-sector coordination body, the Sustainable Workloads 
and Inference Technology Coordination Hub (SWITCH). It brings together public agencies exploring 
how to use AI in public services. SWITCH is considering a shared framework for allocating inference

3
Requirements
Within this scenario, your team advises SWITCH. Develop and assess an intelligent routing strategy 
that balances the usefulness of AI responses with the resources required to produce them. Your solution 
should address the following aspects:
•
Establish a framework for comparing the quality, computational cost, response time, and environ
-
mental impact of AI responses across different types of requests. How can answer quality or task 
difficulty be estimated when a correct answer is not always available for direct comparison?
•
Design and evaluate an adaptive AI router that decides whether a request should use a lightweight 
model, a more capable model, or additional inference-time computation. Investigate how the router 
could learn from previous outcomes, recognise uncertainty, or revise an unsatisfactory response. 
Compare its performance with simpler, non-adaptive alternatives.
•
Explore how routing decisions should change when demand, server availability, and electricity 
carbon intensity vary. Consider whether some requests should be processed at a different time or 
location, and identify the trade-offs between answer quality, waiting time, and carbon emissions. 
Under what conditions does more sophisticated routing cease to be worthwhile?
•
Test the robustness of your proposed system when requests become more difficult, predictions 
are unreliable, or usage grows unexpectedly. Could an apparently efficient router increase total 
emissions or systematically disadvantage certain types of requests? Identify the information and 
evidence needed to evaluate its claims of environmental benefit.
Teams may use accessible AI models, public benchmarks, published measurements, or reproducible 
simulations, provided that assumptions and limitations are clearly explained. The central focus 
should remain on the intelligent decision-making mechanism and its evaluation, rather than carbon 
accounting alone.
4
Share Your Insights
Prepare a one-page, non-technical recommendation memo for SWITCH’s governing board. Explain 
whether the proposed routing strategy should be adopted, what makes its decisions better than a 
fixed model-selection policy, and what trade-offs or failure modes participating public agencies should 
monitor. Present your principal findings in a form suitable for both technical management and an 
informed public audience.

Glossary
Adaptive Inference:
 Adjusting the model or amount of computation used for a request according to 
its estimated needs.
Model Cascade:
 A strategy in which requests may pass through multiple AI models, with more 
capable models invoked when necessary.
Quality Estimation:
 Predicting how useful or reliable a model’s answer is likely to be, possibly 
without access to a definitive correct answer.
Grid Carbon Intensity:
 The greenhouse gas emissions associated with generating a unit of electricity, 
commonly expressed in gCO₂e/kWh.
Rebound Effect:
 An increase in usage following an efficiency improvement that offsets some or all 
of the expected resource savings.
